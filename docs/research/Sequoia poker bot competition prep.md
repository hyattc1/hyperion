# Tables, not solvers, win Sequoia's millisecond poker

Four of the premises behind this question are wrong, and the corrections rewrite the preparation strategy. The event is Sequoia's "No Limit: A Bot Building Tournament" (hosted with OpenAI and Citadel Securities), announced October 27, 2026, and **no round has been played yet**: the first regional is **Menlo Park on Friday November 6** (registration closes Tuesday November 3 at 11:59 PM PT), then Cambridge, MA on November 9, Pittsburgh on November 12, and Finals in San Francisco on November 15 ([Official rules](https://pokerbot.sequoiacap.com/rules.html)). The bot **must be Python**, must **return every decision within one millisecond** (an error or timeout auto-folds, or checks when possible), runs sandboxed with no network inside a 100 MB file cap, and plays **10,000 eight-bot sit-and-gos per scored tournament** starting at 2,000 chips with 10/20 blinds rising every 25 hands, capped at 200 hands, scored by finishing position with **ICM** splitting the remaining points among survivors; six tournaments T1–T6 are weighted 1 through 6, and the **poker variant is secret until the build phase begins** ([Official rules](https://pokerbot.sequoiacap.com/rules.html)). Those constraints answer the three questions directly. (1) Research cannot uncover "the solution" because the game itself is unknown until 6:00 PM on event day, but it can locate the ceiling precisely: one millisecond in Python forbids search, sixty minutes forbids training, and a secret variant invalidates any pre-solved blueprint, so the best bot anyone can field is a crash-free, ICM-aware, short-stack-literate, opponent-adaptive **lookup-and-arithmetic bot**, and the evidence from MIT Pokerbots, ACPC and the local-best-response literature says such a bot beats hastily built CFR in short student fields. (2) Language is not a choice; compiled languages matter only as offline table generators. (3) The highest-EV preparation is, in order: a decision wrapper that can never time out or return an illegal action; a rehearsed interface-adaptation drill so a legal bot is submitted within fifteen minutes; a push/fold and ICM engine for the short-stack phase that every table reaches; precomputed preflop equity tables and position-aware charts; per-seat opponent counters that classify within 10–20 observations; a small postflop rule layer; a local 8-max SNG harness stocked with archetype bots; and a variant-mutation drill. Skip CFR blueprints entirely. One material question is unresolved: the rules neither permit nor forbid carried-in code and tables, so the plan below is split into a tier that is defensible under any reading and a tier to deploy only if the organizers confirm it, and the answer should be requested from pokerbot@sequoiacap.com now.

## The ceiling is a lookup bot, and research reaches it but cannot pass it

The honest answer to "can we find the best possible solution ahead of time" is that the best possible solution is bounded by three constraints that no amount of research relaxes, and that those bounds are low enough that preparation can reach them. The first bound is runtime. One millisecond in CPython is enough for dictionary lookups, arithmetic and a handful of hand evaluations, and nothing else; MIT's 2024 GTO Wizard entry already treated 30 seconds per 1,000 hands as "only a few milliseconds per action" and built a depth-limited solver around it ([gtowizard-ai/mitpoker-2024](https://github.com/gtowizard-ai/mitpoker-2024)), and the Sequoia budget is an order of magnitude tighter. The second bound is training time. The cheapest published equilibrium bot that holds its own against Slumbot is Modicum, whose blueprint alone took **700 core-hours** and still lost 11 ± 8 mbb/g; Pluribus's six-max blueprint took **12,400 core-hours** ([Modicum](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1805.08195); [Meta AI](https://ai.meta.com/blog/pluribus-first-ai-to-beat-pros-in-6-player-poker/)). A hobbyist Linear-MCCFR blueprint trained for about 30 core-hours lost **714.5 ± 331.5 mbb/hand** to Slumbot ([noregrets](https://github.com/All4Eli/noregrets)), and the 2017 MIT champion's CFR bot, trained six days on a laptop with 169/100/100/100 buckets and two bet sizes, "never converged" ([Fro116/Pokerbots](https://github.com/Fro116/Pokerbots)). The third bound is information: any blueprint carried into the room was trained on the wrong game, because the variant, point distribution, blind schedule and interface are revealed at build start ([Official rules §3.2–3.3](https://pokerbot.sequoiacap.com/rules.html)), and MIT's history shows how far a "variant" can drift from hold'em, from secretly permuted ranks (2020) and three simultaneous boards (2021) to a sealed-bid auction for a third hole card (2024), bounties (2025) and three hole cards with a face-up discard (2026) ([engine-2020](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/mitpokerbots/engine-2020/master/engine.py); [class-resources-2025 variant](https://github.com/mitpokerbots/class-resources-2025/blob/main/variant.pdf); [class-resources-2026 variant](https://github.com/mitpokerbots/class-resources-2026/blob/main/variant.pdf)).

What makes this ceiling good news is that lookup bots beat hastily built equilibrium bots in exactly this kind of field. OpenHoldem's hand-written expert rule bot scored **+57 mbb/h against Slumbot** over 100,000 hands while the same group's CFR-based static bot scored −20 ([OpenHoldem](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2012.06168)). Local best response found every top ACPC 2016 no-limit bot exploitable by **more than 3,000 mbb/g**, four times worse than folding every hand, with card abstraction the main cause and most of the exploitation realized "with only using a single pot-bet option" ([Lisý & Bowling](https://arxiv.org/abs/1612.07547)). MIT Pokerbots' organizers say bots "that exploit opponent weaknesses, especially those using the most poker theory and heuristics, tend to perform best," and that an all-in bot was once "hard to counter in real time" ([The Tech](https://thetech.com/2024/02/08/pokerbots)). The rigorous number on exploiting weak bots is also instructive about *how*: against an always-call bot, a best response that waits until all cards are dealt and shoves when ahead captures about 50 BB/hand, whereas shoving as soon as it is ahead on the flop gains only 33; against a bot choosing uniformly random legal actions, fold/call/pot/all-in on the last two streets wins about 80.7 BB/hand because the bot folds half its hands to any all-in ([Lisý & Bowling](https://arxiv.org/abs/1612.07547)). A profiled equity bot in MIT 2024 roughly tripled its win rate against a calling station (+1,008 to +2,861 BB/100), turned an all-in opponent from +61 to +2,002, and gained nothing measurable against tight-aggressive or nitty opponents ([abh-win](https://github.com/abh-win/auction-poker-bot)). That last point defines the ceiling's shape: the edge in this competition comes from harvesting the weak majority and not donating to the strong minority, not from out-solving anyone.

The format pushes the objective toward survival as much as exploitation, which is unfamiliar ground for the likely field. ACPC's organizers designed two scoring rules precisely because they reward different bots: total bankroll "depends on spotting opponents' weaknesses and exploiting them," while in instant run-off "it is more important to defend one's chip stack," and in 2010 the no-limit total-bankroll winner (Tartanian4) finished only third under run-off ([IEEE Spectrum](https://spectrum.ieee.org/a-texas-hold-em-tournament-for-ais); [ACPC 2010](https://poker.cs.ualberta.ca/news_2010.html)). Finishing-position scoring with ICM sits nearer the run-off end: a chip leader holding half the chips is worth about 45% of the pool rather than 50%, and ICM tells leaders to call shoves less often and mid-stacks to tighten ([PokerNews](https://www.pokernews.com/strategy/icm-boosts-tournament-equity-minimizes-risk-46536.htm)). Against a field in which some seats crash, time out or shove randomly, early chip-EV coin flips are negative in points because those seats will bust themselves and promote everyone who is still seated. No source tests this on bot fields directly; it is standard ICM logic applied to a field with a high self-destruction rate. The other side of the same coin is that the MIT and CMU communities that will dominate the Boston and Pittsburgh rooms have six years of reflexes for heads-up, stacks-reset-every-hand, 1,000-hand matches ([engine-2026](https://github.com/mitpokerbots/engine-2026); [cmudsc.com](https://cmudsc.com/pokerai-2026/)), and no evidence surfaced of any student event using multiway tables or ICM. Short-stack and ICM literacy is therefore a genuine differentiator, not table stakes.

Multiway play also removes the theoretical comfort of equilibrium: Pluribus's authors state their techniques "do not have known strong theoretical guarantees on performance outside of the two-player zero-sum setting," and the goal there was empirical ([LessWrong read-through](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/6qtq6KDvj86DXqfp6/let-s-read-superhuman-ai-for-multiplayer-poker)). In an eight-seat game with ICM payouts there is no "GTO" to approximate in the first place, which is one more reason the ceiling is a well-tuned heuristic policy rather than a solver output.

## Python is mandated, so the millisecond decides what runs inside it

Since the runtime language is fixed, the engineering question becomes what Python can do in one millisecond on an unknown sandbox CPU, with an unknown measurement method (wall versus CPU time, grace period or not; the rules do not say). The honest answer is: table lookups, closed-form arithmetic, and a small number of hand evaluations. Pure-Python evaluators are fast enough for the last of these: `treys` benchmarks at about **3.2 million evaluations per second** and PokerKit at about 1.0 million on a laptop core ([PokerKit docs](https://pokerkit.readthedocs.io/en/latest/evaluation.html)), so a decision that evaluates the hero's hand and a few hypothetical runouts costs microseconds. C-extension evaluators are faster and currently maintained, with `phevaluator` 0.6.0 shipping wheels for CPython 3.10–3.14 (Apache-2.0, released July 2026) and `eval7` 0.1.11 shipping wheels through 3.15 with range parsing and equity helpers (MIT, single maintainer, self-described alpha) ([PyPI phevaluator](https://pypi.org/project/phevaluator/); [PyPI eval7](https://pypi.org/project/eval7/)). But whether the sandbox allows any third-party package, including numpy, is not stated anywhere public, so the decision path must work with the standard library alone, with a vendored pure-Python evaluator in the submission and the C extension used only if it imports.

What does not fit is anything sampled or solved on the fly. A worst-case ±1% equity estimate needs about **9,600 Monte Carlo samples** (±2% needs ~2,400; derived from the Wald interval at 95%) ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binomial_proportion_confidence_interval)); at `treys` speed the evaluations alone take ~6 ms and the Python dealing loop realistically pushes a 10k-sample estimate to 50–300 ms, versus roughly 1 ms in C++ or Rust where the ~270M evaluations per second of OMPEval make dealing the bottleneck ([OMPEval](https://github.com/zekyll/OMPEval)). The MIT 2024 profiled bot's 170 µs per 1,500-sample estimate and 160–235 µs per decision were C++ numbers ([abh-win](https://github.com/abh-win/auction-poker-bot)). A full Malmuth-Harville ICM over eight seats is also too slow to run per decision in Python (eight paid places means 8! = 40,320 permutation terms, by my arithmetic tens of milliseconds), so ICM must be cached per hand or approximated with a coarse table; the starter kit's ICM calculator is for scoring and should be treated as reference, not as a per-decision call ([Official rules §3.3](https://pokerbot.sequoiacap.com/rules.html)). Exhaustive river enumeration against one unknown hand (990 opponent combinations) is borderline at roughly 0.3–1 ms in pure Python and should not be on the critical path.

The 100 MB cap is roomy for the right assets and fatal for the wrong ones. The Two Plus Two evaluator's `HandRanks.dat` is about **124 MB** and does not fit ([pokerlookup](https://docs.rs/crate/pokerlookup/latest)), whereas PHEvaluator's 7-card tables are 144 kB ([PokerHandEvaluator](https://github.com/HenryRLee/PokerHandEvaluator)). A 169×169 heads-up all-in equity matrix is **114 KB** as float32 and a 169-class table of equity against one to eight random opponents is about 5 KB; both can live in source as literals ([derived from the 169-class decomposition](https://www.pokerstrategy.com/forum/poker-tools-1361/how-does-equilab-calculate-preflop-range-vs-range-equity-so-fast-523836/)). Flop and turn strength tables are also feasible: there are 1.29 million suit-isomorphic flop situations and 13.96 million turn situations ([MaxSmd/poker-ai](https://github.com/MaxSmd/poker-ai)), so a one-byte hand-strength value per flop situation is under 2 MB and per turn situation about 14 MB, by my arithmetic; the river's 123 million situations do not fit and should be handled by made-hand category plus the small heads-up enumeration when time allows. The caveat is that none of these tables are valid if the variant changes hand rankings or the number of hole cards, which is why the generator matters more than the artifact.

Compiled languages still earn their place offline. OMPEval enumerates and Monte-Carlos equity at 2–10x EquiLab's per-thread speed ([OMPEval](https://github.com/zekyll/OMPEval)); PHEvaluator enumerates all 7-card hands at 60M/s and is still being pushed as of October 2026 ([PokerHandEvaluator](https://github.com/HenryRLee/PokerHandEvaluator)); `rs_poker` 5.1.0 bundles an evaluator, range parser and Monte Carlo in one Apache-2.0 crate ([crates.io](https://crates.io/api/v1/crates/rs_poker)). A 169×169 matrix at 100k samples per matchup is on the order of tens of seconds per core with OMPEval and hours in pure Python, so the table generator should be C++/Rust/numba and parameterized by hand-ranking function and hole-card count, so that it can be re-run in minutes during the build hour if the variant permits. One more Python-specific point: because the rule is fold-on-timeout, a bot that is slow when it holds a strong hand throws away exactly its best spots, so the latency target should be set far below 1 ms (a local p99 under 0.2 ms, with allocation-free decision code, tables loaded at import, and garbage collection disabled or frozen around the decision) rather than at it.

## Pre-build eight assets ranked by expected value, and skip the blueprint

The ranking below weighs each asset by how much score it protects or earns under the 1 ms / Python / secret-variant / 8-max-ICM constraints, how likely it is to survive an unknown variant, and how cheaply it integrates. Items are split by whether they are defensible as "knowledge and public documentation" under the strictest reading of the rules (tier A) or only if carried-in code and tables are confirmed permitted (tier B); the final section explains that split.

| Rank | Asset | Why it ranks here | Variant-robust? | Tier |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Crash-proof decision wrapper with latency guard and legal-action clamp | Every error or timeout is a forced fold; the most common one-hour-build loss mode | Yes | A (can be retyped from memory) |
| 2 | Interface-adaptation drill: adapter pattern rehearsed against a fake kit, first legal submission in ≤15 min | Frees the five iteration windows worth 95% of the score | Yes | A |
| 3 | Short-stack engine: push/fold ranges by stack-in-BB, position and players left; ICM pressure adjustments; cached ICM | Every table passes through 10–20 BB; the field is unfamiliar with ICM | Mostly (preflop structure usually survives) | B for tables, A for method |
| 4 | Preflop tables and charts: 169-class equity vs N opponents, 169×169, position-aware open/3-bet/call thresholds for 8-max | Preflop is the only street where equity can be fully tabled | Only if rankings and hole-card count hold; keep the generator | B |
| 5 | Per-seat opponent counters: VPIP, PFR, shove rate, fold-to-bet, never-folds and dead-seat flags; Beta priors; two exploit dials | Profiling triples win rate vs stations, nothing vs good bots; needs only tens of observations | Yes | A/B |
| 6 | Postflop rule layer: made-hand category, draw flags, flop/turn strength tables, fcpa action set, "wait vs stations, small bets vs folders" | Captures most exploitable value with a tiny action set | Rules yes, tables no | B |
| 7 | Local 8-max SNG harness with blind schedule, position points plus ICM, seeded/duplicate deals, archetype bots, 1 ms profiler | Turns 20-minute windows into A/B tests instead of guesses | Yes | B (own code) |
| 8 | Variant-mutation drill and parameterized evaluator | Converts the secret-variant risk into a known adaptation routine | Yes by design | A |
| Skip | CFR/MCCFR blueprint for the full game | Off-variant, off-stack-depth, no multiway guarantee, 30 core-hours still −715 vs Slumbot, 100 MB cap | No | — |

The wrapper comes first because it is free and because every source agrees the failure mode is real. The rules convert errors and timeouts to folds or checks ([Official rules §3.7](https://pokerbot.sequoiacap.com/rules.html)); MIT's student newspaper reports that the biggest challenge for most students was "the limits of the homemade engine," not poker ([The Tech](https://thetech.com/2024/02/08/pokerbots)); the strongest documented MIT entries shipped "0 state mismatches" as a metric ([abh-win](https://github.com/abh-win/auction-poker-bot)); and a Zeniteq write-up makes the sharp observation that "a sophisticated policy that often times out gives up control exactly when it matters" ([Zeniteq](https://www.zeniteq.com/sequoia-and-openai-open-a-100k-pokerbot-tournament-6aycef)). The wrapper is a try/except around the whole decision, a raise-size clamp to legal bounds, a validation of the chosen action against the legal set, a fallback to check-else-fold, and a deadline check that returns the safe action if a soft budget is exceeded. It should be rehearsed until it can be typed in five minutes without reference.

The adaptation drill is second because the interface is unknown and the clock is unforgiving. The two unofficial rehearsal repos that exist both say the same thing: `mack-dot/poker-gym` ships a `drills/fake_kit/` exercise "for adapting to an unseen kit" and an `AgentAdapter` enforcing a 1 ms deadline and legality, and `amalkoch/pokerbot-lab` recommends a timed 60-minute build drill, stateless policies using the supplied RNG, and measuring latency separately from chip results; both state they are not the official kit ([poker-gym](https://github.com/mack-dot/poker-gym); [pokerbot-lab](https://github.com/amalkoch/pokerbot-lab)). The pattern to practice is a thin adapter that translates the kit's observation into an internal state (hole cards, board, stacks, pot, legal actions, blind level, position, players remaining) and translates an internal action back, so that all strategy code is kit-agnostic. The goal is a tight, legal, never-crashing bot submitted inside fifteen minutes, which also serves as the permanent fallback.

The short-stack engine is third because the blind structure guarantees its relevance. Starting stacks are 100 BB and blinds rise every 25 hands, about three orbits at eight-handed, with at most eight levels before the 200-hand cap ([Official rules §4.4](https://pokerbot.sequoiacap.com/rules.html)); the exact schedule is in the kit. HoldemResources publishes exact heads-up Nash push/fold solutions from 1 to 200 BB in 0.05 BB steps as a downloadable raw data zip, with the caveat that push-or-fold "beyond about 10bb is usually a bad idea" as a sole strategy ([HoldemResources](https://www.holdemresources.net/hune)). Heads-up ranges are too wide for eight seats; no multiway push/fold table with numbers was found, so the multiway adjustment has to be built, either by hand-tightening from the heads-up tables or, as a stretch, by running a small MCCFR on the all-in-or-fold abstraction of the eight-seat game with the actual blind schedule, which is the one place CFR earns its keep here because the game is tiny (169 classes × stack buckets × seats) and the payoff structure can include ICM. The exploitative overlay is cheap: bots that call shoves too wide make the correct response tighter shoving and much tighter calling than Nash, and bots that shove randomly fold half their range to a re-shove ([Lisý & Bowling](https://arxiv.org/abs/1612.07547)). ICM should be computed at most once per hand from stacks and cached, or replaced by a coarse risk-premium table keyed by the number of shorter stacks and the point distribution announced at build start.

Preflop tables are fourth. No trustworthy downloadable 169×169 all-in equity matrix or free machine-readable GTO opening set for this format was found, so both must be generated: the matrix with OMPEval or phevaluator offline, and the opening, three-bet and calling thresholds by hand from equity ranks and position, since the only free range sets located were six-max cash charts, which are not this game ([OMPEval](https://github.com/zekyll/OMPEval); [pokertrainer.se](https://pokertrainer.se/preflop-introduction/)). The practitioner guidance to tighten preflop against calling stations rather than copy their looseness, and to cut continuation-bet frequency sharply with each extra caller, should be encoded as position- and opponent-count-aware parameters rather than fixed ranges ([Card Player](https://www.cardplayer.com/cardplayer-poker-magazines/66346-commerce-casino-30-2/articles/22972-combatting-calling-stations); [DeucesCracked](https://www.deucescracked.com/blog/continuation-bet-strategy-2026-nlhe-guide)).

Opponent counters are fifth because the table scale fits them and because the evidence says they pay where it matters. With state reset per table, at most 200 hands and seven anonymous opponents ([Official rules §3.8](https://pokerbot.sequoiacap.com/rules.html)), only preflop entry statistics and gross frequencies converge: practitioners treat VPIP and PFR as usable at 20 hands, continuation-bet and fold-to-bet stats at 100 opportunities, and river stats at 1,000 ([BlackRain79](https://www.blackrain79.com/2017/11/poker-hud-stat-sample-size.html)). The academic recipe for tiny samples is a weak equilibrium-flavored prior and an early switch to exploitation: Hoehn et al. found learning to exploit beats a pessimistic Nash strategy from about hand 50, robust across 50–400-hand games, with weak priors (2 fictitious observations) clearly beating strong ones ([Hoehn et al.](https://cdn.aaai.org/AAAI/2005/AAAI05-123.pdf)); DBBR blended observed frequencies with a prior worth five observations ([Ganzfried & Sandholm 2011](https://www.cs.huji.ac.il/~jeff/aamas11/papers/B4_R56.pdf)); and safe-exploitation theory says to risk only what has been won in expectation against that opponent ([Ganzfried & Sandholm 2015](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~sandholm/safeExploitation.teac15.pdf)). Deterministic bots have no within-player variance, so they should classify faster than humans do, though no source measures this. Operationally the model is a handful of Beta-smoothed counters per seat, classification after 10–20 opportunities, and two output dials: bluff frequency (zero against stations and maniacs, high against folders) and value-bet threshold (lower against stations and maniacs, higher against nits) ([PokerNews](https://www.pokernews.com/strategy/alex-fitzgerald-nits-stations-and-maniacs-43259.htm)). Two flags are specific to this format and cheap: a seat that folds to every bet but never when checked to is probably timing out or crashing, and shoving into it when it faces a bet is pure profit; and a seat whose actions show no dependence on anything is a random bot, worth about 80 BB/hand to late-street all-ins ([Lisý & Bowling](https://arxiv.org/abs/1612.07547)).

The postflop layer is sixth because a tiny action set captures most of the available value. Fold, check-call, pot and all-in chosen by a strength proxy against pot odds, with size escalating by street and by opponent fold tendency, is enough: most of LBR's exploitation of top CFR bots used a single pot-sized bet, and against never-folders the money is made by waiting until the river ([Lisý & Bowling](https://arxiv.org/abs/1612.07547)). The 2017 MIT prize winner's postflop was win-rate tables generated in Python (river first, turn as the average over 46 rivers) and consumed by interval thresholds, tuned after scrimmages to bet smaller for value against CFR bots "which tended to fold to large raises" ([SPAM_pokerbot_2017](https://github.com/sebLopezCot/SPAM_pokerbot_2017)). Solver-informed c-bet baselines from training sites (50–85% in position on dry boards at 25–33% pot, 30–35% out of position, under 40% on wet boards) are reasonable defaults before any read exists ([DeucesCracked](https://www.deucescracked.com/blog/continuation-bet-strategy-2026-nlhe-guide)).

The harness is seventh only because the kit ships a local simulator ([Official rules §3.3](https://pokerbot.sequoiacap.com/rules.html)); its value is the archetype bots and the metrics that the kit will not have. The MIT 2024 profiled bot's method is the template: a rules-exact engine, baseline bots (TAG, station, nit, maniac, random, all-in), and results with 95% confidence intervals over tens of thousands of hands ([abh-win](https://github.com/abh-win/auction-poker-bot)). For this format the harness must score by finishing position with ICM at the cap, include a timeout bot and a copy of the hero (filler seats are copies of submitted bots, so self-play robustness matters ([Official rules §4.6](https://pokerbot.sequoiacap.com/rules.html))), use seeded and duplicate deals because single seeds "swing by hundreds of mbb/hand" ([noregrets](https://github.com/All4Eli/noregrets)), and assert the 1 ms budget per decision with a margin. `mack-dot/poker-gym` is a pure-Python starting point that already has Malmuth-Harville ICM, a 1 ms adapter and a 17-rule mutation pool for rehearsing secret variants, with the caveat that it is one person's guess at the format ([poker-gym](https://github.com/mack-dot/poker-gym)).

The variant drill is eighth because its payoff is insurance rather than edge. The evaluator and table generator should be parameterized by rank mapping, hand-category ordering and hole-card count, and the team should have rehearsed what to do for each of MIT's historical twists: permuted ranks (regenerate tables with the permutation), extra or fewer hole cards (fall back to made-hand heuristics and a quick Monte Carlo at import time if import time is unmetered), bounties or auctions (treat as a side payoff in the shove/call thresholds). For twists that break the 169-class abstraction entirely, the fallback is the tier-A bot: tight preflop by a hand-authored rule, push/fold by stack depth, no bluffs, and the opponent counters.

The item to skip is the thing a game-theory-literate team is most tempted to build. A full-game blueprint is trained on the wrong variant and the wrong stack depths, has no theoretical guarantee multiway, needs to fit a 100 MB file, and at laptop compute budgets is still hundreds of mbb/hand behind Slumbot while remaining exploitable by any opponent that bets odd sizes on late streets ([noregrets](https://github.com/All4Eli/noregrets); [Lisý & Bowling](https://arxiv.org/abs/1612.07547)). Depth-limited search, river re-solving, Deep CFR, DeepStack-style value nets and ReBeL are all ruled out by the millisecond alone ([Modicum](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1805.08195); [DeepStack](https://arxiv.org/abs/1701.01724)). If the team has spare days, the only CFR worth running is the small all-in-or-fold multiway solve described under item three.

## The build hour decides 5% of the score; the five windows after it decide 95%

The weights make the evening a sequence of integration sprints rather than one build. The first fifteen minutes should go to reading the variant, point distribution and blind schedule, wiring the adapter, and submitting the tier-A fallback bot, because the first accepted submission is what plays T1 if anything later breaks and because "a working baseline competes while logic improves" ([Zeniteq](https://www.zeniteq.com/sequoia-and-openai-open-a-100k-pokerbot-tournament-6aycef)). Minutes fifteen to forty should plug in the short-stack engine and preflop tables, regenerated if the variant allows, and run the harness against the archetype set with the latency profiler on. Minutes forty to fifty-five should add the postflop layer and opponent counters. The last five minutes should not introduce anything untested: a bot frozen at T1 that crashes costs only 1/21 of the score, but the same mistake at T6 costs 6/21, and the DBBR experience that exploitation "decreases significantly for the first several hundred hands" when the prior misleads it is a warning against shipping an untested adaptive change late ([Ganzfried & Sandholm 2011](https://www.cs.huji.ac.il/~jeff/aamas11/papers/B4_R56.pdf)).

Each 20-minute window allows at most four submissions ([Official rules §3.5](https://pokerbot.sequoiacap.com/rules.html)), and only the last accepted one before the freeze plays. The discipline is A/B against the harness with duplicate deals, promote only on a win with a confidence interval, and keep the previous bot's file ready to resubmit. The field itself will drift across the evening: T1 and T2 will contain more crashing, random and all-in bots because many teams will have shipped something in sixty minutes, while by T5 and T6 the survivors will have fixed crashes and tightened up. The exploit mix should drift with it, from harvesting stations and random shovers early toward ICM-disciplined survival and selective pressure late, and the counters make that shift automatic within each table even though no learning carries across tables ([Official rules §3.8](https://pokerbot.sequoiacap.com/rules.html)). Because every opponent is another team's fresh bot and empty seats are copies of submitted bots ([Official rules §4.6](https://pokerbot.sequoiacap.com/rules.html)), the distribution of opponents is literally the distribution of what students build in an hour, which the MIT evidence describes as heuristic bots, equity-threshold bots that behave like nits postflop, and occasional all-in bots ([The Tech](https://thetech.com/2024/02/08/pokerbots)).

Two format details deserve explicit handling in the code. First, a timeout "checks if checking is available" ([Official rules §3.7](https://pokerbot.sequoiacap.com/rules.html)), so a dead seat is not free money when it is checked to; the gift materializes only when it faces a bet, which argues for betting into suspected dead seats rather than checking behind. Second, disputes must be raised with a Gamemaster before the next scored tournament, and T6 disputes within five minutes of results ([Official rules §6.2](https://pokerbot.sequoiacap.com/rules.html)), so someone on the team should be watching results between tournaments rather than only coding.

## Pre-written code is neither allowed nor banned, so prepare two tiers and ask now

The rules constrain the bot, not the inputs to the build. They prohibit "colluding with other teams, copying another team's code, interfering with the platform, or using the submission endpoint in unintended ways," plus malware, credentials, fraud and sharing the variant before the event ends ([Official rules §6.1, §6.4](https://pokerbot.sequoiacap.com/rules.html)). They permit "Astra and Codex to write, debug, and refine their bots during the build phase" and state that "publicly available documentation is permitted," that OpenAI tools are "optional at every stage," and that participants "must comply with applicable third-party software and data licenses" ([Official rules §3.4, §7.3, §8.4](https://pokerbot.sequoiacap.com/rules.html)). Nothing in the text addresses a personal library, precomputed tables, or bringing your own environment, and the 100 MB cap would be an odd number to specify if only hour-old code were expected. Against that, every piece of marketing says "from scratch" ([Sequoia on X](https://x.com/sequoia/status/2115111389501218868)), Noam Brown's framing is that coding agents make it "possible to build a poker bot in an *hour*" ([Noam Brown on X](https://x.com/polynoamial/status/2115125429619343609)), prizes and advancement "remain subject to verification, including code review" ([Official rules §5.4](https://pokerbot.sequoiacap.com/rules.html)), and one unofficial prep repo warns that "prewritten code may not be allowed in the competition, so you should confirm this with the organizer" ([pokerbot-lab](https://github.com/amalkoch/pokerbot-lab)). The naming of Astra and Codex specifically, rather than "any AI assistant," also leaves it ambiguous whether a non-OpenAI assistant is tolerated during the build; the safest reading is that OpenAI tools are the sanctioned path and others are unaddressed rather than banned.

The right response is to email pokerbot@sequoiacap.com today with three precise questions (may teams bring their own helper code and precomputed tables; may teams use non-OpenAI coding assistants; is any third-party Python package available in the sandbox) and to prepare for either answer. Tier A, defensible under the strictest reading, is everything that lives in the team's heads and in public documentation: the wrapper pattern, the adapter pattern, the ICM and push/fold logic, the opponent-counter design, the postflop rules, and the generator scripts' design, all rehearsed until they can be re-derived with an assistant in minutes. Tier B, deployed only if confirmed, is the artifacts: the equity tables, the push/fold data, the harness, the archetype bots and the vendored evaluator. Under tier A the realistic sixty-minute output is still a tight, ICM-aware, counter-adjusting bot with hand-authored ranges, which is most of the ceiling; under tier B it is the full stack with tables regenerated for the variant.

## Conclusion

The brief imagined a research problem: find the strongest known poker algorithm and compress it into an hour. The actual event is an engineering problem with a known shape and an unknown surface. The shape is fixed by three numbers that no amount of cleverness changes, one millisecond, sixty minutes and eight seats with ICM, and those numbers make the strongest feasible bot a well-parameterized lookup policy that never forfeits a decision, plays short stacks correctly, and reads seven anonymous opponents within a few orbits. The surface, the variant and the interface, is deliberately hidden until 6:00 PM, which means the competition is really a test of how much of the bot was decided before the door opened and how fast the rest can be wired. Preparation therefore should optimize for regeneration speed and adaptation routine, not for the quality of any single pre-solved artifact, and the team that arrives with a rehearsed adapter, a generator that rebuilds its tables in minutes, and a harness that scores exactly as the organizers do will spend its hour on integration while most of the room is still debugging the kit.

Two implications follow for what to do between now and November 6. First, the likely field's expertise is heads-up cash-game poker, and nothing in the MIT or CMU ecosystems trains multiway tournament judgment; ICM discipline and short-stack ranges are therefore the cheapest real edge available, worth more than any postflop sophistication. Second, this report is a pre-event snapshot and should be re-run after each regional: rule 6.4 bars participants from sharing the variant only until that event ends, so Menlo Park's variant, kit interface and winning approaches may surface on the evening of November 6, before Boston on November 9 and Pittsburgh on November 12, and whether later regionals reuse the same variant is itself unknown. A team competing in Boston or Pittsburgh should budget a research pass on November 7 and treat whatever it finds as the single most valuable input to its tier-A design.
